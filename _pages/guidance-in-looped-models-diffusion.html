Guidance can dramatically improve prompt following and sample quality in image and video generation, simply by changing how a trained model is sampled.1 Classifier-free guidance (CFG) is a standard ingredient in Stable Diffusion 32 and Wan3. Its benefits are valuable enough that FLUX.1 [dev] is trained to distill guidance directly into the model.4

Can language models benefit in the same way? There is a catch: applying guidance to each next-token prediction does not generally produce the desired guided joint distribution over the full token sequence. The two procedures normalize probabilities differently. Looped models offer a way to narrow this gap.5, 6, 7, 8 When predictions from different loops are close, guidance changes token preferences at first order, while the normalization error enters at second order. In this blog, we explain why loops can make guidance more natural for language models, and how this connects them to diffusion.

01Why diffusion guidance needs no normalizer

Start with a conditional density \(p(x\mid c)\), where \(c\) might be a text prompt. Suppose we want to favor samples that are especially characteristic of that condition. One way to express this preference is to tilt the density:

\[ \widetilde p_w(x\mid c) =\frac{1}{Z_w(c)}\, \frac{p(x\mid c)^{1+w}}{p(x)^w},\qquad w\geq0. \tag{1} \]

The ratio \(p(x\mid c)/p(x)\) favors samples that are more likely under the prompt than without conditioning. At \(w=0\), we recover ordinary conditional generation. Increasing \(w\) strengthens that preference. The normalizer \(Z_w(c)\) makes the density integrate to one.

Diffusion models use the score, the gradient of a log density, to guide denoising. Following common practice, we compute the score without knowing the normalizer, as the normalizer \(Z\) is considered independent of \(x\)9. Guidance can therefore be written as a combination of the conditional and unconditional scores:

\[ \nabla_x\log\widetilde p_w(x\mid c) =(1+w)\nabla_x\log p(x\mid c) -w\nabla_x\log p(x). \tag{2} \]

This is the score combination behind classifier-free guidance1. In this score formulation, the normalizer vanishes.*

02Token-level guidance and the joint distribution

Guidance already exists in autoregressive LLMs. Stay on topic with Classifier-Free Guidance10 applies CFG by contrasting next-token predictions with and without the conditioning prompt, while keeping the generated prefix. Contrastive decoding11 uses a weaker model as the reference; DoLa12 uses predictions from earlier layers. Here we study their shared core idea: combine two next-token predictions, then normalize.

The joint distribution assigns a probability to every complete token sequence. Applying guidance to that distribution means reweighting each sequence and normalizing once. Token-level guidance instead normalizes separately at each prefix. These operations generally produce different sequence probabilities.

As Sander noted in his 2022 blog post:

“my hunch is that diffusion guidance works so well precisely because we are able to apply it to the joint distribution”

Sander Dieleman, Guidance: a cheat code for diffusion models9 (2022)

Let \(p_K\) be the predictor we want to strengthen and \(p_E\) its reference. For a sequence \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)\) of fixed length \(T\), \(y_t\) is the next token and \(y_{<t}=(y_1,\ldots,y_{t-1})\) is its prefix. Assuming positive token probabilities and leaving the fixed prompt conditioning implicit, token-level guidance gives

\[ q(y_t\mid y_{<t})=\frac{1}{C(y_{<t})}\, \frac{p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})^{1+w}}{p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})^w}, \qquad C(y_{<t})=\sum_{y_t}\frac{p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})^{1+w}}{p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})^w}. \]

Here \(C(y_{<t})\) is the local normalizer; the sum runs over all possible next tokens.

Write \(p_K(y)\) and \(p_E(y)\) for each model's sequence probability, obtained by multiplying its token probabilities. Both \(Q_{\rm joint}\) and \(Q_{\rm local}\) are distributions over complete sequences, but they apply guidance differently:

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_{\rm joint}(y) &=\frac{1}{Z}\, \frac{p_K(y)^{1+w}}{p_E(y)^w}, \qquad Z=\sum_y\frac{p_K(y)^{1+w}}{p_E(y)^w},\\[4pt] Q_{\rm local}(y) &=\frac{1}{\prod_{t=1}^T C(y_{<t})}\, \frac{p_K(y)^{1+w}}{p_E(y)^w}. \end{aligned} \tag{3} \]

The numerators are identical, but the normalizers differ: \(Z\) is shared by every sequence, whereas the product of local normalizers depends on the sequence's prefixes \(y_{<t}\). This is the normalization mismatch.

Additionally, computing the full-sequence normalizer \(Z\) exactly is impractical for real LLMs: it requires summing over all possible future token sequences, whose number grows exponentially with length.

If the guided joint distribution is our target, practical token-level guidance therefore tolerates a normalization error. The local rule avoids summing over future sequences by normalizing only the next-token scores. The question is how to make the resulting error small.

03Loooop

A looped Transformer applies the same block of layers repeatedly before predicting the next token. Huginn5 and Ouro6 implement this idea in different ways. Each completed loop gives a hidden state that can be converted into next-token logits (unnormalized token scores): \(z_E\) after an earlier loop \(E\), and \(z_K\) after the final loop \(K\).

The earlier prediction serves as the weaker reference for guiding the final prediction. Both come from the same model on the same text prefix; the difference is how many loops of computation they use.

LoopCD13 uses these predictions for guidance without additional training. Its logit variant strengthens the final logits \(z_K\) along the difference \(z_K-z_E\). Applying softmax gives \(q\), the guided next-token probability distribution:

\[ z_{\rm guided}=z_K+w(z_K-z_E),\qquad q=\operatorname{softmax}(z_{\rm guided}). \tag{4} \]

LoopCD's logit variant uses the first loop as its reference. The paper also combines hidden states before converting them into logits; the analysis below concerns the logit rule in Equation (4).

Because every loop repeats the same computation, its predictions are expected to stay close as the hidden state is refined. This gives us a route back to the normalization problem: close loop predictions can make token-level guidance a better approximation to the guided joint distribution. Below we show how the error depends on their logit difference, then measure that difference.

Why loops help

The key assumption is that predictions from different loops have similar logits. Under this small-difference assumption, we can bound the local normalizer \(C(y_{<t})\).

At a fixed prefix, write \(\delta=z_K-z_E\). After removing an irrelevant constant offset, assume \(\delta=\epsilon d\), with \(d\) bounded and \(\epsilon\) small. Keep the guidance strength fixed and bounded. A Taylor expansion gives

\[ \boxed{\log C(y_{<t}) =\frac{w(1+w)}{2}\operatorname{Var}_{p_E}(\delta) +O(\epsilon^3).} \tag{5} \]

The constant and first-order terms cancel. Guidance changes token log-odds at first order; its local log-normalization cost starts at second order.

Proof of Equation (5)

Fix a prefix \(y_{<t}\). The local normalizer satisfies the exact identity

\[ \begin{aligned} \log C(y_{<t}) &=D_{\rm KL}\bigl(p_K(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\Vert q(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\bigr)\\ &\quad+wD_{\rm KL}\bigl(p_K(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\Vert p_E(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\bigr). \end{aligned} \tag{6} \]

Proof of Equation (6). For each possible next token \(y_t\), rearrange the definition of \(q(y_t\mid y_{<t})\):

\[ \log\frac{p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})}{q(y_t\mid y_{<t})} =\log C(y_{<t})-w\log\frac{p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})}{p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})}. \]

Taking the expectation over \(y_t\sim p_K(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\) yields Equation (6). For \(w\geq0\), both KL terms are nonnegative, so \(\log C(y_{<t})\geq0\). This identity does not require small logit differences. \(\square\)

For the Taylor expansion, write the logit difference as \(\delta(y_t\mid y_{<t})=z_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})-z_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})\). Define its log moment-generating function under the reference distribution:

\[ \psi(\alpha;y_{<t}) =\log\sum_{y_t}p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})e^{\alpha\delta(y_t\mid y_{<t})}. \]

Softmax gives

\[ p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t}) =p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})e^{\delta(y_t\mid y_{<t})-\psi(1;y_{<t})}. \]

Substituting into the definition of \(C(y_{<t})\), we obtain the exact expression

\[ \log C(y_{<t}) =\psi(1+w;y_{<t})-(1+w)\psi(1;y_{<t}). \]

For \(\delta=\epsilon d\) with uniformly bounded \(d\), and bounded \(\alpha\), expand

\[ \begin{aligned} \psi(\alpha;y_{<t}) &=\alpha\mathbb E_{p_E(\cdot\mid y_{<t})}[\delta]\\ &\quad+\frac{\alpha^2}{2}\operatorname{Var}_{p_E(\cdot\mid y_{<t})}(\delta) +O(\epsilon^3). \end{aligned} \]

The expectation and variance are over next tokens \(y_t\) at the same prefix \(y_{<t}\). Substitution cancels the linear terms and leaves

\[ \begin{aligned} \log C(y_{<t}) &=\frac{(1+w)^2-(1+w)}{2}\operatorname{Var}_{p_E(\cdot\mid y_{<t})}(\delta) +O(\epsilon^3)\\ &=\frac{w(1+w)}{2}\operatorname{Var}_{p_E(\cdot\mid y_{<t})}(\delta) +O(\epsilon^3). \end{aligned} \]

This proves Equation (5). \(\square\)

Suppose \(0\leq\log C(y_{<t})\leq\eta\) at every prefix of a length-\(T\) sequence. Equation (3) then bounds the log-probability difference between token-level guidance and the guided joint distribution:

\[ \left|\log\frac{Q_{\rm local}(y)}{Q_{\rm joint}(y)}\right| \leq T\eta. \tag{7} \]

Equation (7) bounds the full-sequence error by \(T\eta\). Under the uniform small-difference assumption, \(\eta=O(\epsilon^2)\), so this error is \(O(T\epsilon^2)\): close predictions let the token-level rule approximate the guided joint distribution without summing over future sequences.

Proof of Equation (7)

Set \(D(y)=\prod_t C(y_{<t})\). Equation (3) gives \(ZQ_{\rm joint}(y)=Q_{\rm local}(y)D(y)\). Summing over sequences gives

\[ Z=\sum_y Q_{\rm local}(y)D(y) =\mathbb E_{Q_{\rm local}}D(Y). \]

The prefix bound gives \(1\leq D(y)\leq e^{T\eta}\) for every sequence. Its expectation obeys the same bounds, so \(0\leq\log Z\leq T\eta\). Finally,

\[ \log\frac{Q_{\rm local}(y)}{Q_{\rm joint}(y)} =\log Z-\log D(y). \]

Both terms lie in \([0,T\eta]\), establishing Equation (7). This argument retains the global normalizer rather than dropping it.

This is why similar loop outputs help: guidance changes token preferences at first order in \(\epsilon\), while the mismatch between token-level guidance and the guided joint distribution is \(O(T\epsilon^2)\) for a sequence of length \(T\).

04Are predictions from different loops actually closer?

We compared two looped models, Huginn-0125 and Ouro-2.6B, with two ordinary Transformers, Qwen2.5-3B and Pythia-2.8B. Each model saw the same 1,024 text prefixes: eight positions on each of 128 GSM8K development questions. At each prefix, we compared the model's next-token logits partway through its computation with its own final logits.

Here, depth \(k\) means the number of completed loops in a looped model, or completed layers in an ordinary Transformer. The final depth \(K\) stays fixed. Let \(V\) be the vocabulary size and \(v\in\{1,\ldots,V\}\) a candidate token's index. Then \(z_{k,v}(y_{<t})\) is that token's score after depth \(k\), on the same text prefix \(y_{<t}\).

For each \(k\), compute the change to the final score, \(\delta^{(k)}_v(y_{<t})=z_{K,v}(y_{<t})-z_{k,v}(y_{<t})\). Remove its vocabulary mean, \(\bar\delta^{(k)}(y_{<t})=V^{-1}\sum_v\delta^{(k)}_v(y_{<t})\), because adding the same constant to every logit does not change softmax. We measure the remaining change with

\[ \operatorname{RMS}_{\rm centered}(k,y_{<t}) =\sqrt{\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^V \bigl(\delta^{(k)}_v(y_{<t})-\bar\delta^{(k)}(y_{<t})\bigr)^2}. \tag{8} \]

Intuitively, this measures how much the next-token scores change between an intermediate depth and the final depth. It is the standard deviation of those score changes across the vocabulary. A smaller value means the predictions vary less as the model performs more loops or layers.

In the plot, we vary \(k\), keep the prefix and final depth \(K\) fixed, and average the RMS over the 1,024 prefixes. The horizontal position is \(k/K\): 50% means halfway through that model. The depths used for the quarter, half, and three-quarter comparisons are:

The looped models' mean gaps are 2.8–14.1 times smaller across all twelve comparisons. Every paired 95% bootstrap interval for the ratio lies below one. The ordering also holds after scale normalization and when using only question prefixes, before any answer text.

The smaller logit gaps support the proposed mechanism: loop predictions stay closer, making smaller normalization errors plausible. Under the small-difference assumption, guidance changes token preferences at first order while the normalization error grows only at second order. This gives looped models a natural route toward effective token-level guidance that stays close to the desired joint distribution.

05Loops and diffusion, beyond guidance

Guidance is one connection between looped models and diffusion; learning to refine a prediction is the broader one. Autoguidance uses a smaller, less-trained diffusion model as a reference,14 while a looped model can use its own earlier prediction. In both cases, a weaker prediction provides information for improving a stronger one.

These connections are now explicit in the methods themselves. Time-Modulated Looped Transformers15 borrow diffusion-style timestep conditioning, and LoopFormer7 adds step-size conditioning and shortcut consistency. Denoising Recursion Models16 learn multi-step correction of corrupted solutions, while Thinking with Looped Flows17 couples recurrent states to a learned denoising flow. In the other direction, Looped Diffusion Language Models18 and Looped-DiT19 repeat Transformer blocks inside diffusion models, allowing compute to grow through loop depth as well as sampling steps.

06What's next?

In this blog, we showed that intermediate and final logits are much closer in the looped models we tested. This supports an approximate decomposition of sequence-level guidance into per-token guidance: close logits reduce the normalizer mismatch, helping explain why token-level guidance can work so well in looped models.

The same structure offers flexibility at test time. Rather than one fixed computation, a looped model performs a sequence of smaller refinement steps. An inference algorithm can decide how long to keep refining a state and how strongly to steer each update.

The broader picture concerns test-time scaling itself. Test-time scaling is about how computation is organized, not only how much is spent.20 Loops refine a hidden state; diffusion refines a sample. Guidance adds another use for their intermediate predictions: a direction for correction. More compute is useful when the inference algorithm knows what to do with it. What scales is the model together with that algorithm.

* Score-based models learn scores of noisy distributions from samples.21 Combining conditional and unconditional score estimates implements the reweighting idea at each noise level, although the resulting sampler need not reproduce the corresponding clean-data distribution in Equation (1).9,22,23 We therefore view score-level CFG as a practical surrogate for distribution-level reweighting. ↩
