Guidance is an inference-time technique that steers generation toward desired outputs without updating model weights. In diffusion, classifier-free guidance amplifies the difference between conditional and unconditional predictions to strengthen the conditioning signal. Looped language models offer a related form of self-guidance: contrast an early prediction with a later prediction from the same computation.

The useful question is not whether we can subtract two predictions. It is what distribution that subtraction produces.

The answer comes down to normalization. In diffusion, the normalizer is constant with respect to the entire sample. In autoregressive language modeling, it depends on the prefix, which is itself part of the answer. Loops make the two predictions more closely related, but they do not automatically erase that distinction.

In brief. Shared computation gives a looped model its own reference predictor. This makes guidance a natural inference-time intervention. Under a small-logit-difference assumption, the normalization mismatch is second order for a fixed, bounded guidance strength. That is a useful approximation, not a proof that ordinary token-by-token decoding has become diffusion.

01Why diffusion can do guidance

Definition: classifier-free guidance (CFG)

The distributional aim of classifier-free guidance is to favor samples that more strongly support a condition \(c\), such as a text prompt. For clean data \(x\), start with the conditional distribution \(p_0(x\mid c)\) and reweight by its density ratio to the unconditional distribution \(p_0(x)\). The desired tilted conditional distribution is:

\[ \pi_0(x)=\frac{p_0(x\mid c)^{1+w}p_0(x)^{-w}}{Z_0}. \]

Here \(c\) is fixed, \(Z_0\) is the finite normalizing constant, and \(w\geq 0\) controls the extra preference for condition-consistent samples. At \(w=0\), we recover the ordinary conditional distribution. This is the target that motivates guidance; the next step is to apply the same tilting idea at each noise level and derive its score.

At noise level \(t\), apply the same reweighting to the noisy-data distribution: multiply \(p_t(x\mid c)\) by \(p_t(c\mid x)^w\) and normalize. Call this tilted density \(\widetilde\pi_t\). Bayes' rule converts the condition likelihood into a ratio of conditional and unconditional densities.

Write \(s_c=\nabla_x\log p_t(x\mid c)\) and \(s_u=\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\) for the conditional and unconditional scores. Absorb the condition-only factor \(p(c)^w\) into the normalizer \(Z_t\), then take the log-gradient of the rewritten density:

\[ \begin{gathered} p_t(c\mid x)=\frac{p_t(x\mid c)\,p(c)}{p_t(x)},\\[0.5em] \widetilde\pi_t(x)=\frac{p_t(x\mid c)^{1+w}p_t(x)^{-w}}{Z_t},\\[0.5em] \begin{aligned} s_g&=\nabla_x\log\widetilde\pi_t(x)\\ &=(1+w)\nabla_x\log p_t(x\mid c)\\ &\quad-w\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\\ &=(1+w)s_c-ws_u. \end{aligned} \end{gathered} \]

Because \(Z_t\) is constant with respect to \(x\), its log-gradient vanishes. The score combination is therefore a consequence of the desired reweighting: first specify the tilted density, then differentiate its log. With exact conditional and unconditional scores, this gives a valid score for \(\widetilde\pi_t\) at each noise level, without computing \(Z_t\). We use that score in the diffusion sampler to steer generation toward the condition.*

In practice, the model estimates \(s_c\) and \(s_u\) at the same noisy sample and noise level, with and without the condition. Their difference supplies the guidance direction associated with the condition likelihood. No separate classifier is needed; the score rule is the implementation of this distributional idea, not an arbitrary subtraction of predictions.[1]

02Why LLM doesn't support guidance natively

More precisely: an autoregressive LLM can use guidance, but guiding each next token is not the same as guiding the joint distribution of complete answers. Let \(s=y_{<t}\) be the already-generated prefix, \(p_+\) the strong predictor, and \(p_-\) the reference. Assume both predictors put positive probability on the tokens being compared.

The per-token guidance rule is straightforward: for each candidate next token \(a\), take the strong model's log-probability and add \(w\) times its difference from the reference model's log-probability. Applying softmax to these guided log-probabilities gives \(q(a\mid s)\), the distribution used to sample the next token. Specifically, exponentiation produces the unnormalized score \(r(a\mid s)\), and dividing by its sum \(C_w(s)\) makes the probabilities sum to one:

\[ \begin{aligned} r(a\mid s)&=p_+(a\mid s)^{1+w}p_-(a\mid s)^{-w},\\ q(a\mid s)&=\frac{r(a\mid s)}{C_w(s)},\\ C_w(s)&=\sum_a r(a\mid s). \end{aligned} \]

Let \(P_+(y)\) and \(P_-(y)\) be the probabilities of a complete answer \(y\) under the strong and reference models. Define \(Q^\star\) as the target sequence-level guided distribution: weight each complete answer by \(P_+(y)^{1+w}P_-(y)^{-w}\), then normalize once over all complete answers.

The single normalizing constant \(Z\) is the sum of these weights over all complete answers, assumed finite. In contrast, \(Q_{\mathrm{local}}\) is the distribution obtained by multiplying the per-token normalized probabilities \(q(y_t\mid y_{<t})\). The two distributions are:

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_{\mathrm{local}}(y)&=\frac{P_+(y)^{1+w}P_-(y)^{-w}}{\prod_t C_w(y_{<t})},\\ Q^\star(y)&=\frac{P_+(y)^{1+w}P_-(y)^{-w}}{Z}. \end{aligned} \]

The mismatch is between the distribution we actually sample, \(Q_{\mathrm{local}}\), and the target distribution, \(Q^\star\). They have the same numerator, but different denominators: \(Z\) is the same for every complete answer, whereas \(\prod_t C_w(y_{<t})\) generally varies from one answer to another because each answer follows a different sequence of prefixes.

For example, two answers with equal guided weights have equal probability under \(Q^\star\). Under \(Q_{\mathrm{local}}\), the answer with the smaller product of token normalizers receives more probability. Both distributions are normalized; the problem is that per-token normalization can change the relative probabilities of complete answers, rather than merely rescaling all their weights by one common constant.

The missing term is lookahead

To recover the target distribution in Equation (4), we need to ask: how can we guide each next-token decision so that the resulting complete answer follows \(Q^\star\)? The required next-token distribution is \(Q^\star(a\mid s)\), not just the locally normalized score \(q(a\mid s)\).

Choosing \(a\) selects a whole branch of complete answers, all beginning with \(sa\). Its probability must equal the target probability mass of that entire branch, conditional on \(s\). We must therefore sum over what could come after \(a\), not just score \(a\) itself. This is the motivation for lookahead.

Definition: lookahead

Lookahead means accounting for the possible future continuations of a candidate token when deciding whether to choose it now, rather than judging that token only by its immediate score.

Here, its exact value is \(V(sa)\): the total unnormalized guided weight of all completions after appending token \(a\) to prefix \(s\). It sums over all futures, not just one predicted or best continuation, and is not a probability.

Conditioning on \(s\) cancels both the shared past-token factors and the global normalizer \(Z\). The remaining weight of the branch beginning with \(a\) is \(r(a\mid s)V(sa)\): the current score times the total weight of its possible futures. With \(z\) ranging over remaining completions, this gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} V(s)&=\sum_z\prod_i r(z_i\mid s z_{<i}),\\[0.5em] Q^\star(a\mid s)&=\frac{r(a\mid s)V(sa)}{V(s)},\\ V(s)&=\sum_b r(b\mid s)V(sb). \end{aligned} \]

Ordinary per-token guidance for an LLM, as defined in Equation (3), omits the lookahead factor \(V(sa)\) and therefore need not recover the target sequence distribution \(Q^\star\).

A two-token counterexample

Both models choose \(A\) or \(B\) with probability 0.5. After \(A\), both are uniform over \(1,2\). After \(B\), the strong model predicts \((0.9,0.1)\), while the reference stays at \((0.5,0.5)\). At \(w=1\), per-token guidance still chooses \(B\) with 50% probability, but \(Q^\star\) requires 62.1%: it accounts for the extra guided weight in the continuations after \(B\).

Shared benchmarks, not a controlled architecture comparison. Both literature tables below use GSM8K and HumanEval, but the models, prompts, guidance references, and decoding protocols differ. Each change compares guidance with its own model's unguided baseline; cross-paper differences do not isolate the effect of looping.

Per-token guidance can already improve ordinary LLMs. The lookahead argument identifies a distributional mismatch; it does not say that local guidance cannot improve answers.

03Loop models enable guidance with shared hidden states

Looped models, however, offer a way to mitigate this mismatch. If their predictions at different loop depths have small logit differences, the error from omitting lookahead can be small, as we show below.

Guidance in looped models: LoopCD

A looped model reuses the same block before generating each token. Readouts after \(E\) and \(K>E\) loops give predictions \(p_E\) and \(p_K\) for the same prefix. The earlier readout provides a weaker reference without another model. Apple's LoopCD uses this contrast for inference-time guidance, without retraining.[6]

LoopCD-Logits. Read out both states, then extrapolate away from the early logits. The paper uses the first loop as the reference, \(E=1\):

\[ \begin{gathered} z_g=z_K+w(z_K-z_E),\\ \operatorname{softmax}(z_g)\propto p_K^{1+w}p_E^{-w}. \end{gathered} \]

LoopCD-Hidden. Apply \(h_g=h_K+w(h_K-h_E)\) before the shared output layers. The probability identity above need not hold when those layers are nonlinear. Our analysis below concerns the logit variant.

These results do not establish that loop guidance is stronger. They show model- and task-dependent effects. The analysis below offers a conditional explanation of normalization error, not an empirical architecture ranking.

In a separate seven-benchmark multiple-choice study, half-depth LoopCD matches or exceeds full-depth baselines with 22.5–48.2% fewer theoretical forward FLOPs, not measured wall-clock savings.[6]

Why loops help

The key is not that two readouts come from adjacent loops. All loop iterations reuse the same block and output head, so their representations share a common coordinate system. The early and late readouts used for guidance may be many iterations apart.

Assumption. For the same prefix, the readouts used for guidance have small logit differences: \(z_K=z_E+\epsilon u\), with small \(\epsilon\) and bounded \(u\). This is an empirical assumption about output variation across loop depths, not a requirement that the depths be adjacent. Sharing a representation space motivates checking this assumption; it does not by itself establish it.

To see why this assumption matters, return to the earlier normalization problem. Per-token guidance differs from the sequence-level target through the prefix-dependent factors \(C_w(s)\). We therefore need to connect these factors to the variation between loop outputs.

With the later predictor \(p_K\) as the strong model and the earlier predictor \(p_E\) as the reference, the normalizer separates into the change caused by guidance and the disagreement between loop predictions:

\[ \begin{aligned} \log C_w(s)&=\underbrace{\operatorname{KL}(p_K\Vert q)}_{\text{guidance-step size}}\\ &\quad+\underbrace{w\operatorname{KL}(p_K\Vert p_E)}_{\text{loop disagreement}}. \end{aligned} \]
Derivation of Equation (7)

Fix the prefix \(s\) and suppress the token arguments. All expectations below are over tokens sampled from \(p_K\). Starting from Equation (3):

\[ \begin{gathered} q=\frac{p_K^{1+w}p_E^{-w}}{C_w(s)},\\[0.7em] \log\frac{p_K}{q}=\log C_w(s)-w\log\frac{p_K}{p_E},\\[0.7em] \operatorname{KL}(p_K\Vert q) =\mathbb E_{p_K}\!\left[\log\frac{p_K}{q}\right]\\ =\log C_w(s)-w\mathbb E_{p_K}\!\left[\log\frac{p_K}{p_E}\right]\\ =\log C_w(s)-w\operatorname{KL}(p_K\Vert p_E). \end{gathered} \]

The normalizer is constant over tokens at this fixed prefix. Moving the second KL term to the other side gives Equation (7).

Both terms are nonnegative. Under the small-\(\epsilon\) assumption above, and with \(w\) fixed and bounded, the constant and linear terms cancel:

\[ \begin{aligned} \log C_w&=\tfrac12 w(1+w)\epsilon^2\operatorname{Var}_{p_E}(u)\\ &\quad+O(\epsilon^3). \end{aligned} \]
Derivation of Equation (9)

Fix the prefix and let \(z_K=z_E+\epsilon u\). Softmax converts this logit difference into an exponential reweighting of the earlier predictor:

\[ \begin{gathered} p_K(a)=\frac{p_E(a)e^{\epsilon u(a)}}{\mathbb E_{p_E}[e^{\epsilon u}]},\\[0.7em] C_w=\sum_a p_K(a)^{1+w}p_E(a)^{-w}=\frac{\mathbb E_{p_E}[e^{(1+w)\epsilon u}]}{\bigl(\mathbb E_{p_E}[e^{\epsilon u}]\bigr)^{1+w}}. \end{gathered} \]

For bounded \(u\), the log of its exponential moment has the following Taylor expansion around \(t=0\). Its constant term is zero because \(\log\mathbb E_{p_E}[1]=0\):

\[ \log\mathbb E_{p_E}[e^{tu}]=t\mathbb E_{p_E}[u]+\tfrac12 t^2\operatorname{Var}_{p_E}(u)+O(t^3). \]

Take logs of the normalizer above, then substitute the expansion at \(t=(1+w)\epsilon\) and \(t=\epsilon\). The linear coefficient cancels, while the quadratic coefficient simplifies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \log C_w&=\log\mathbb E_{p_E}[e^{(1+w)\epsilon u}]-(1+w)\log\mathbb E_{p_E}[e^{\epsilon u}]\\[0.4em] &=\tfrac12\bigl[(1+w)^2-(1+w)\bigr]\epsilon^2\operatorname{Var}_{p_E}(u)+O(\epsilon^3)\\[0.4em] &=\tfrac12 w(1+w)\epsilon^2\operatorname{Var}_{p_E}(u)+O(\epsilon^3). \end{aligned} \]

This gives Equation (9). With \(w\) fixed and bounded, both Taylor remainders remain \(O(\epsilon^3)\).

What about the sequence normalizer?

Equation (9) controls the local normalizer \(C_w(s)\); it does not remove the global normalizer \(Z\). For answers of fixed length \(T\), \(Z\) is the lookahead value at the empty prefix, or equivalently the average of the path-normalizer products:

\[ \begin{aligned} Z&=V(\varnothing)\\ &=\mathbb E_{y\sim Q_{\mathrm{local}}} \!\left[\prod_{t=1}^{T}C_w(y_{<t})\right]. \end{aligned} \]

Suppose \(0\leq\log C_w(s)\leq\delta\) at every relevant prefix. Each path product lies between \(1\) and \(e^{T\delta}\), so its average \(Z\) does too. Combining this with Equation (4) bounds the mismatch for every complete answer:

\[ \begin{gathered} 0\leq\log Z\leq T\delta,\\[0.4em] \left|\log\frac{Q^\star(y)}{Q_{\mathrm{local}}(y)}\right|\leq T\delta. \end{gathered} \]

When \(T\delta\) is small, the per-token sampling distribution and the target sequence-level distribution in Equation (4) assign nearly the same probability to each complete answer. With fixed \(w\), uniformly bounded \(u\), and the small-logit-difference assumption holding across prefixes, \(\delta=O(\epsilon^2)\). The whole-sequence log-probability-ratio bound scales as \(O(T\epsilon^2)\), reflecting accumulation across \(T\) tokens. Guidance changes token log-odds at first order, \(O(w\epsilon)\).

Derivation of Equations (13) and (14)

Substitute \(r=qC_w\) into the total weight of all complete answers and sum over them to obtain Equation (13). The expectation lies in the same interval \([1,e^{T\delta}]\) as every path product. Taking logs of Equation (4) gives:

\[ \begin{gathered} \log\frac{Q^\star(y)}{Q_{\mathrm{local}}(y)}\\ =\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log C_w(y_{<t})-\log Z. \end{gathered} \]

Both the sum and \(\log Z\) lie in \([0,T\delta]\), so their difference lies in \([-T\delta,T\delta]\). This proves Equation (14).

How loops relate to diffusion

Both reuse a learned model to iteratively refine a continuous state. A diffusion sampler repeatedly uses a denoising model to update a noisy sample; a looped language model reuses the same block to update its hidden state for a fixed prefix. In diffusion, the iteration is tied to a noise level; in a looped model, it controls how much computation is spent before the next token is emitted.

The connection to guidance is the ability to contrast compatible predictions. CFG compares conditional and unconditional predictions at the same noisy state and noise level; loop guidance compares early and later readouts at the same prefix. Both amplify a preferred prediction relative to a reference, but obtain that reference differently: by changing conditioning in CFG, and by changing computation depth in loop guidance.

This analogy does not make a loop iteration a diffusion step. Weight sharing alone does not supply a denoising objective or make a hidden-state update a score, and the per-prefix normalizer still exists. The result above is narrower: under the small-logit-difference assumption and at fixed guidance strength, loop guidance produces a first-order change in log-odds with a second-order normalization cost.

A more diffusion-like extension would train loops to denoise a representation of the whole answer and revise all positions before decoding; this is a possible future design, not a requirement for the per-token guidance discussed here.

What the "cheatcode" actually buys. A looped model can supply its own computationally weaker reference, making self-guidance a natural intervention without a second separately trained predictor. The shared state space makes the contrast meaningful; small differences between readouts can make it locally well controlled. This transfers the prediction-contrast idea from diffusion without assuming that recurrent computation is itself a diffusion sampler.

What would test this mechanism?

At tokens where guidance changes the answer, compare candidate continuation values. Roll out completions under \(Q_{\mathrm{local}}\), accumulate each completion's future \(\log C_w\), and take a log-mean-exp across rollouts to estimate \(\log V(sa)\). If differences between candidates are small compared with the guidance shift, the local approximation is effective at those decisions.

Record \(\operatorname{KL}(p_K\Vert q)\) and \(w\operatorname{KL}(p_K\Vert p_E)\) separately. Computing the normalizers uses distributions already available to guided decoding, but the diagnostic continuation rollouts themselves require additional inference. This separates a good depth pair from simply making the guidance step smaller.
