Guidance can dramatically improve prompt following and sample quality in image and video generation, simply by changing how a trained model is sampled.1 Classifier-free guidance (CFG) is a standard ingredient in Stable Diffusion 32 and Wan3. Its benefits are valuable enough that FLUX.1 [dev] is trained to distill guidance directly into the model.4

Can language models benefit in the same way? There is a catch: applying guidance to each next-token prediction does not generally produce the desired guided joint distribution over the full token sequence. The two procedures normalize probabilities differently. Looped models offer a way to narrow this gap. When predictions from different loops are close, guidance changes token preferences at first order, while the normalization error enters at second order. In this blog, we explain why loops can make guidance more natural for language models, and how this connects them to diffusion.

01Why diffusion guidance needs no normalizer

Start with a conditional density \(p(x\mid c)\), where \(c\) might be a text prompt. Suppose we want to favor samples that are especially characteristic of that condition. One way to express this preference is to tilt the density:

\[ \widetilde p_w(x\mid c) =\frac{1}{Z_w(c)}\, \frac{p(x\mid c)^{1+w}}{p(x)^w},\qquad w\geq0. \tag{1} \]

The ratio \(p(x\mid c)/p(x)\) favors samples that are more likely under the prompt than without conditioning. At \(w=0\), we recover ordinary conditional generation. Increasing \(w\) strengthens that preference. The normalizer \(Z_w(c)\) makes the density integrate to one.

Diffusion models use the score, the gradient of a log density, to guide denoising. Following common practice, we compute the score without knowing the normalizer, as the normalizer \(Z\) is considered independent of \(x\)5. Guidance can therefore be written as a combination of the conditional and unconditional scores:

\[ \nabla_x\log\widetilde p_w(x\mid c) =(1+w)\nabla_x\log p(x\mid c) -w\nabla_x\log p(x). \tag{2} \]

This is the score combination behind classifier-free guidance1. In this score formulation, the normalizer vanishes.*

02Token-level guidance and the joint distribution

Guidance already exists in autoregressive LLMs. Stay on topic with Classifier-Free Guidance8 applies CFG by contrasting next-token predictions with and without the conditioning prompt, while keeping the generated prefix. Contrastive decoding9 uses a weaker model as the reference; DoLa10 uses predictions from earlier layers. Here we study their shared core idea: combine two next-token predictions, then normalize.

The joint distribution assigns a probability to every complete token sequence. Applying guidance to that distribution means reweighting each sequence and normalizing once. Token-level guidance instead normalizes separately at each prefix. These operations generally produce different sequence probabilities.

As Sander noted in his 2022 blog post:

“my hunch is that diffusion guidance works so well precisely because we are able to apply it to the joint distribution”

Sander Dieleman, Guidance: a cheat code for diffusion models5 (2022)

Let \(p_K\) be the predictor we want to strengthen and \(p_E\) its reference. For a sequence \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)\) of fixed length \(T\), \(y_t\) is the next token and \(y_{<t}=(y_1,\ldots,y_{t-1})\) is its prefix. Assuming positive token probabilities and leaving the fixed prompt conditioning implicit, token-level guidance gives

\[ q(y_t\mid y_{<t})=\frac{1}{C(y_{<t})}\, \frac{p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})^{1+w}}{p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})^w}, \qquad C(y_{<t})=\sum_{y_t}\frac{p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})^{1+w}}{p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})^w}. \]

Here \(C(y_{<t})\) is the local normalizer; the sum runs over all possible next tokens.

Write \(p_K(y)\) and \(p_E(y)\) for each model's sequence probability, obtained by multiplying its token probabilities. The guided joint distribution uses a single global normalizer \(Z\); token-level guidance instead multiplies the locally normalized probabilities \(q\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_{\rm joint}(y) &=\frac{1}{Z}\, \frac{p_K(y)^{1+w}}{p_E(y)^w}, \qquad Z=\sum_y\frac{p_K(y)^{1+w}}{p_E(y)^w},\\[4pt] Q_{\rm local}(y) &=\frac{1}{\prod_{t=1}^T C(y_{<t})}\, \frac{p_K(y)^{1+w}}{p_E(y)^w}. \end{aligned} \tag{3} \]

The numerators are identical, but the normalizers differ: \(Z\) is shared by every sequence, whereas the product of local normalizers depends on the sequence's prefixes \(y_{<t}\). This is the normalization mismatch.

How would the guided joint distribution choose each token?

Every joint distribution admits a sequential factorization. The issue is whether the simple local rule gives us the right conditionals. For this derivation, abbreviate the unnormalized token weight as \(a(y_t\mid y_{<t})=p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})^{1+w}/p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})^w\). Let \(H(y_{<t})\) be the total unnormalized guided weight of all completions after that prefix. Writing \(y_{\leq t}=(y_1,\ldots,y_t)\) for the prefix including the next token, the exact conditional is

\[ Q_{\rm joint}(y_t\mid y_{<t}) =\frac{a(y_t\mid y_{<t})H(y_{\leq t})}{H(y_{<t})}. \tag{4} \]

The term \(H(y_{\leq t})\) accounts for all the futures opened up by choosing \(y_t\). Ordinary local guidance omits it. Computing it exactly is usually impractical, because it requires summing over possible continuations.

For a complete sequence, \(H(y)=1\). Grouping completions by their next token gives

\[ H(y_{<t})=\sum_{y_t}a(y_t\mid y_{<t})H(y_{\leq t}),\qquad H(\varnothing)=Z. \]

Divide the joint target's probability of the extended prefix by that of the original prefix. The weights of already chosen tokens and the global normalizer cancel:

\[ \frac{Q_{\rm joint}(y_{\leq t})}{Q_{\rm joint}(y_{<t})} =\frac{a(y_t\mid y_{<t})H(y_{\leq t})}{H(y_{<t})}. \]

This gives Equation (4). The local rule agrees whenever \(H(y_{\leq t})\) is the same for every possible next token at that prefix.

Additionally, computing the full-sequence normalizer \(Z\) exactly is impractical for real LLMs: it requires summing over all possible future token sequences, whose number grows exponentially with length.

If the guided joint distribution is our target, practical token-level guidance therefore tolerates a normalization error. Exact token choices require the future-completion sums in Equation (4); the local rule avoids them by normalizing only the next-token scores. The question is how to make the resulting error small.

A tiny example makes the difference visible

Suppose a sequence has just two tokens. Both predictors choose \(A\) or \(B\) with probability \(1/2\). After \(A\), both remain uniform over \(1,2\). After \(B\), the reference is still uniform but the stronger predictor assigns probabilities \(0.9,0.1\). Token-level guidance cannot change the first decision: the predictors agree there. The guided joint distribution can, because it accounts for the guided weight of the continuations after \(B\).

03Loooop

A looped Transformer applies the same block of layers repeatedly before predicting the next token. Huginn11 and Ouro12 implement this idea in different ways. Each completed loop gives a hidden state that can be converted into next-token logits (unnormalized token scores): \(z_E\) after an earlier loop \(E\), and \(z_K\) after the final loop \(K\).

The earlier prediction serves as the weaker reference for guiding the final prediction. Both come from the same model on the same text prefix; the difference is how many loops of computation they use.

LoopCD13 uses these predictions for guidance without additional training. Its logit variant strengthens the final logits \(z_K\) along the difference \(z_K-z_E\). Applying softmax gives \(q\), the guided next-token probability distribution:

\[ z_{\rm guided}=z_K+w(z_K-z_E),\qquad q=\operatorname{softmax}(z_{\rm guided}). \tag{5} \]

LoopCD's logit variant uses the first loop as its reference. The paper also combines hidden states before converting them into logits; the analysis below concerns the logit rule in Equation (5).

Because every loop repeats the same computation, its predictions are expected to stay close as the hidden state is refined. This gives us a route back to the normalization problem: close loop predictions can make token-level guidance a better approximation to the guided joint distribution. Below we show how the error depends on their logit difference, then measure that difference.

Why loops help

The key assumption is that predictions from different loops have similar logits. Under this small-difference assumption, we can bound the local normalizer \(C(y_{<t})\).

At a fixed prefix, write \(\delta=z_K-z_E\). After removing an irrelevant constant offset, assume \(\delta=\epsilon d\), with \(d\) bounded and \(\epsilon\) small. Keep the guidance strength fixed and bounded. A Taylor expansion gives

\[ \boxed{\log C(y_{<t}) =\frac{w(1+w)}{2}\operatorname{Var}_{p_E}(\delta) +O(\epsilon^3).} \tag{6} \]

The constant and first-order terms cancel. Guidance changes token log-odds at first order; its local log-normalization cost starts at second order.

This is why similar loop outputs help: guidance changes token preferences at first order in \(\epsilon\), while the local log-normalizer grows only at second order.

Derive Equation (6): the exact identity and Taylor expansion

First, the local normalizer satisfies the exact identity

\[ \log C(y_{<t}) =D_{\rm KL}(p_K\Vert q)+wD_{\rm KL}(p_K\Vert p_E). \tag{7} \]

Proof of Equation (7). Suppress the prefix, let \(v\) index possible tokens, and use \(\log q=(1+w)\log p_K-w\log p_E-\log C\). Rearranging gives

\[ \log\frac{p_K(v)}{q(v)} =\log C-w\log\frac{p_K(v)}{p_E(v)}. \]

Taking the expectation over \(v\sim p_K\) yields Equation (7). For \(w\geq0\), both KL terms are nonnegative, so \(\log C(y_{<t})\geq0\). This identity does not require small logit differences. \(\square\)

To find how quickly \(\log C(y_{<t})\) approaches zero when the predictions are close, define the log moment-generating function of the logit difference under the reference distribution:

\[ \psi(\alpha)=\log\mathbb E_{v\sim p_E}e^{\alpha\delta(v)}. \]

Softmax gives \(p_K(v)=p_E(v)e^{\delta(v)-\psi(1)}\). Substituting into the definition of \(C\), we obtain the exact expression

\[ \log C=\psi(1+w)-(1+w)\psi(1). \]

For \(\delta=\epsilon d\) with uniformly bounded \(d\), and bounded \(\alpha\), expand

\[ \psi(\alpha)=\alpha\mu+ \frac{\alpha^2}{2}\sigma^2+O(\epsilon^3), \quad \mu=\mathbb E_{p_E}\delta, \quad \sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}_{p_E}(\delta). \]

The two linear terms are both \((1+w)\mu\). Their difference is zero; the remaining quadratic coefficient is

\[ \frac{(1+w)^2-(1+w)}{2}\sigma^2 =\frac{w(1+w)}{2}\sigma^2. \]

Adding any constant to \(\delta\) adds a linear term to \(\psi\), which cancels in exactly the same way. The relevant assumption concerns variation across tokens, not the arbitrary origin of the logits.

For comparison, the change in log-odds relative to the final predictor is exactly

\[ \log\frac{q(v)}{q(u)}-\log\frac{p_K(v)}{p_K(u)} =w\bigl(\delta(v)-\delta(u)\bigr). \]

For fixed \(w\), this is \(O(\epsilon)\), whereas \(\log C\) begins at \(O(\epsilon^2)\).

Bounding the error over the full sequence

Suppose \(0\leq\log C(y_{<t})\leq\eta\) at every prefix of a length-\(T\) sequence. Equation (3) then bounds the log-probability difference between token-level guidance and the guided joint distribution:

\[ \left|\log\frac{Q_{\rm local}(y)}{Q_{\rm joint}(y)}\right| \leq T\eta. \tag{8} \]

Equation (8) bounds the full-sequence error by \(T\eta\). Under the uniform small-difference assumption, \(\eta=O(\epsilon^2)\), so this error is \(O(T\epsilon^2)\): close predictions let the token-level rule approximate the guided joint distribution without summing over future sequences.

Prove the sequence bound, including the global normalizer

Set \(D(y)=\prod_t C(y_{<t})\). Equation (3) gives \(ZQ_{\rm joint}(y)=Q_{\rm local}(y)D(y)\). Summing over sequences gives

\[ Z=\sum_y Q_{\rm local}(y)D(y) =\mathbb E_{Q_{\rm local}}D(Y). \]

The prefix bound gives \(1\leq D(y)\leq e^{T\eta}\) for every sequence. Its expectation obeys the same bounds, so \(0\leq\log Z\leq T\eta\). Finally,

\[ \log\frac{Q_{\rm local}(y)}{Q_{\rm joint}(y)} =\log Z-\log D(y). \]

Both terms lie in \([0,T\eta]\), establishing Equation (8). This argument retains the global normalizer rather than dropping it.

A finite, non-asymptotic bound is also available. Let \(R=\max_v\delta(v)-\min_v\delta(v)\). In the preceding proof, \(\psi''(\alpha)\) is the variance of \(\delta\) under an exponentially tilted distribution, and is at most \(R^2/4\). The remainder for linear interpolation of \(\psi\) at 0 and 1, evaluated at \(1+w\), gives

\[ 0\leq\log C\leq\frac{w(1+w)}{8}R^2. \]

Thus a uniform range bound \(R(y_{<t})\leq R_*\) yields \(\eta=w(1+w)R_*^2/8\). This makes clear why average RMS alone cannot certify the sequence guarantee. The proof uses a fixed finite horizon and common positive support; unrestricted generation with EOS needs corresponding control of length and the global normalizer.

04Are predictions from different loops actually closer?

We compared two looped models, Huginn-0125 and Ouro-2.6B, with two ordinary Transformers, Qwen2.5-3B and Pythia-2.8B. Each model saw the same 1,024 text prefixes: eight positions on each of 128 GSM8K development questions. At each prefix, we compared the model's next-token logits partway through its computation with its own final logits.

Here, depth \(k\) means the number of completed loops in a looped model, or completed layers in an ordinary Transformer. The final depth \(K\) stays fixed. Let \(V\) be the vocabulary size and \(v\in\{1,\ldots,V\}\) a candidate token's index. Then \(z_{k,v}(y_{<t})\) is that token's score after depth \(k\), on the same text prefix \(y_{<t}\).

For each \(k\), compute the change to the final score, \(\delta^{(k)}_v(y_{<t})=z_{K,v}(y_{<t})-z_{k,v}(y_{<t})\). Remove its vocabulary mean, \(\bar\delta^{(k)}(y_{<t})=V^{-1}\sum_v\delta^{(k)}_v(y_{<t})\), because adding the same constant to every logit does not change softmax. We measure the remaining change with

\[ \operatorname{RMS}_{\rm centered}(k,y_{<t}) =\sqrt{\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^V \bigl(\delta^{(k)}_v(y_{<t})-\bar\delta^{(k)}(y_{<t})\bigr)^2}. \tag{9} \]

Intuitively, this measures how much the next-token scores change between an intermediate depth and the final depth. It is the standard deviation of those score changes across the vocabulary. A smaller value means the predictions vary less as the model performs more loops or layers.

In the plot, we vary \(k\), keep the prefix and final depth \(K\) fixed, and average the RMS over the 1,024 prefixes. The horizontal position is \(k/K\): 50% means halfway through that model. The depths used for the quarter, half, and three-quarter comparisons are:

Measurement details

The models are frozen base checkpoints. Inputs use a common plain-text, eight-shot Q/A format. Answer-prefix positions come from cached Huginn-32 continuations, with no filtering for answer correctness. We selected shared character boundaries that round-trip exactly through all four tokenizers, including the prompt boundary. This matches text, not token IDs across different vocabularies.

Intermediate hidden states are converted into logits using each architecture's existing output computation, including Huginn's final coda layers, normalization, and vocabulary head. We did not fit an additional prediction head. Native forwards use BF16; full-vocabulary statistics use float64 at temperature one. A native-forward check and a future-token control passed at all 84 audited model/depth settings. An independent audit recomputed 28,672 depth-position records.

Intervals resample the 128 questions, retaining each question's eight positions together; paired comparisons resample the same questions for both models. We used 5,000 bootstrap draws. These are individual intervals over the sampled questions, not simultaneous intervals or estimates of variation across training seeds.

The twelve comparisons pair each looped model with each ordinary Transformer at \(k/K=25\%,50\%,75\%\). At half depth, Huginn's gap is 0.268 times Pythia's, with a 95% interval of [0.261, 0.276]; Ouro's is 0.203 times Pythia's, with interval [0.199, 0.209].

The normalized gap divides by the final logits' centered RMS at each position before averaging. Centered RMS weights vocabulary entries uniformly; the variance in Equation (6) weights them by the reference probabilities \(p_E\). The plot is a direct diagnostic of logit similarity. Its vertical axes start at zero, and lines connect measured depths. At \(k=K\), the gap is zero by definition, so that endpoint is omitted.

Depth fractions match relative progress through each model, rather than FLOPs. The checkpoints differ in architecture, training, vocabulary, and size; this comparison measures their predictions using their existing output computations.

LoopCD's first-loop reference deserves separate care: it is 1/32 of the deployed depth for Huginn but 1/4 for Ouro. We therefore do not present “first versus final” as a matched-depth comparison. The source report includes that diagnostic separately.

How accurate is the quadratic approximation itself?

We also computed the exact \(\log C\) and its quadratic approximation. At a quarter depth with \(w=0.5\), the median relative error across the 1,024 positions is 76.9% for Huginn and 75.3% for Ouro, versus 29.2% for Qwen and 27.6% for Pythia. Here relative error means \(|\text{quadratic}-\log C|/\max(\log C,10^{-12})\); the floor is inactive at these positions. The looped models have smaller absolute normalizers in this comparison, but not smaller relative Taylor error.

Even at three-quarter depth, the largest observed absolute centered logit difference is 5.60 for Huginn and 6.44 for Ouro. A small vocabulary-average gap can coexist with larger differences on particular tokens. Equation (8) uses a uniform bound over prefixes, whereas the plot summarizes average logit differences.

The smaller logit gaps support the proposed mechanism: loop predictions stay closer, making smaller normalization errors plausible. Under the small-difference assumption, guidance changes token preferences at first order while the normalization error grows only at second order. This gives looped models a natural route toward effective token-level guidance that stays close to the desired joint distribution.

05Loops and diffusion, beyond guidance

Guidance is one connection between looped models and diffusion; learning to refine a prediction is the broader one. Autoguidance uses a smaller, less-trained diffusion model as a reference,14 while a looped model can use its own earlier prediction. In both cases, a weaker prediction provides information for improving a stronger one.

These connections are now explicit in the methods themselves. Time-Modulated Looped Transformers15 borrow diffusion-style timestep conditioning, and LoopFormer16 adds step-size conditioning and shortcut consistency. Denoising Recursion Models17 learn multi-step correction of corrupted solutions, while Thinking with Looped Flows18 couples recurrent states to a learned denoising flow. In the other direction, Looped Diffusion Language Models19 and Looped-DiT20 repeat Transformer blocks inside diffusion models, allowing compute to grow through loop depth as well as sampling steps.

Test-time-scaling work suggests a sharper perspective: capability depends on how computation is organized, not only on how much is spent.21 Both looped models and diffusion offer test-time scaling through iterative refinement, spending extra computation on hidden-state updates or sampling steps. Their connection is not just that they repeat computation, but that the inference algorithm determines how those updates are used: how many to take, how large they should be, and how guidance steers them. What scales is the model together with its inference algorithm.
