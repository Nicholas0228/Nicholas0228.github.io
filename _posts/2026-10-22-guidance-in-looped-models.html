Looped language models are attracting growing attention as another way to scale reasoning: spend more computation without adding more parameters. These models repeatedly apply shared layers to refine their hidden states before predicting a token, and can outperform non-looped models on reasoning tasks at the same parameter count.1, 2, 11, 21

The looped design also opens up new possibilities for decoding. Standard decoding uses only the final prediction, while guidance uses an earlier prediction as a reference and pushes the final prediction further along the early-to-final direction. Guidance has been a long-studied problem in LLMs, with mixed results.5, 20 In looped models, however, recent work has shown this form of guidance to be particularly effective.3 In this blog, we ask: why does guidance appear to work better in looped models than in standard LLMs? We explain how loops can help, and how this connects language models more closely to diffusion.

01More use from the same loops

A looped Transformer applies the same block of layers repeatedly before predicting the next token. Huginn1 and Ouro2 implement this idea in different ways. For the same text prefix, an early state \(h_E\) and a final state \(h_K\) can each be converted into next-token logits: \(z_E\) and \(z_K\). Logits are the unnormalized scores assigned to the vocabulary.

The difference \(z_K-z_E\) records how the token scores change between the early and final loops. Instead of averaging the two predictions, guidance extrapolates beyond the final one:

\[ z_{\rm guided}=z_K+w(z_K-z_E),\qquad q=\operatorname{softmax}(z_{\rm guided}). \tag{1} \]

Here \(w\geq0\) controls the guidance strength and \(q\) is the guided next-token distribution. At \(w=0\), we use the final prediction unchanged. For \(w>0\), we move further along the early-to-final direction.

LoopCD3 implements this idea without additional training or recurrent loops. Its logit variant uses the first loop as the reference and adds one evaluation of that state's output layers. It can also combine hidden states before the output layers. Both forms improve the published results below, with the number of loops unchanged within each comparison:

The hidden-state result uses loop 7 to guide loop 32 before converting the combined state into logits.3 Our analysis below follows the logit rule in Equation (1).

Using a reference prediction is already familiar in LLM decoding. Contrastive decoding4 compares a stronger model with a weaker one; DoLa5 contrasts predictions from different layers; language-model CFG6 compares predictions with and without prompt conditioning. Loops supply another reference: the same model, on the same prefix, after less computation.

LoopCD reports substantial gains from this simple change. This raises a natural question: what makes looped models particularly well suited to guidance? Below, we connect this behavior to the similarity between predictions across loops. We first examine the gap between the sequence-level distribution we want and the token-level rule we use in practice.

02What does token-level guidance actually sample?

Our target is a guided joint distribution over complete token sequences, favoring sequences that the final predictor prefers relative to the reference. We want to guide the complete sequence, but in practice we apply guidance to one token at a time.

Let \(p_E=\operatorname{softmax}(z_E)\) and \(p_K=\operatorname{softmax}(z_K)\) be the early and final predictors. For a fixed-length sequence \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)\), \(y_t\) is the next token and \(y_{<t}\) its prefix. We analyze a fixed guidance strength \(w\), assume positive token probabilities, and leave the shared prompt implicit.

The practical token-level rule in Equation (1) can be written as:

\[ q(y_t\mid y_{<t})=\frac{1}{C(y_{<t})}\, \frac{p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})^{1+w}}{p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})^w}, \qquad C(y_{<t})=\sum_{y_t}\frac{p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})^{1+w}}{p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})^w}. \tag{2} \]

\(C(y_{<t})\) is the local normalizer. A token gets more weight when the final predictor favors it relative to the early one. The normalizer makes these weights sum to one at the current prefix.

Now compare this practical rule with our sequence-level target. Each predictor assigns a sequence probability by multiplying its next-token probabilities. Reweighting these sequence probabilities and normalizing once defines the target \(Q_{\rm joint}\). Generating token by token from Equation (2) instead produces \(Q_{\rm local}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_{\rm joint}(y) &=\frac{1}{Z}\, \frac{p_K(y)^{1+w}}{p_E(y)^w}, \qquad Z=\sum_y\frac{p_K(y)^{1+w}}{p_E(y)^w},\\[4pt] Q_{\rm local}(y) &=\frac{1}{\prod_{t=1}^T C(y_{<t})}\, \frac{p_K(y)^{1+w}}{p_E(y)^w}. \end{aligned} \tag{3} \]

The numerators match; the normalizers do not. \(Z\) is shared by every sequence. The product of local normalizers depends on which prefixes the generated sequence visits. This is the normalization mismatch.

A two-token example

Suppose a sequence has just two tokens. Both predictors choose \(A\) or \(B\) first with probability \(1/2\). After \(A\), both remain uniform over \(1,2\). After \(B\), the early predictor is still uniform but the final predictor assigns probabilities \(0.9,0.1\). Local guidance leaves the first decision unchanged because the predictors agree there. Joint guidance changes it, because the guided continuations after \(B\) carry more total weight.

The example identifies what we need to control: variation in the local normalizers along a sequence. What would keep these normalizers close to 1?

03Why close loop logits help

The key assumption is that predictions from different loops have similar logits. Under this small-difference assumption, we can bound the local normalizer \(C(y_{<t})\).

At a fixed prefix, write \(\delta=z_K-z_E=\epsilon d\), with \(d\) bounded and \(\epsilon\) small. Keep \(w\) fixed and bounded. The local log-normalizer then has the expansion

\[ \boxed{\log C(y_{<t}) =\frac{w(1+w)}{2}\operatorname{Var}_{p_E}(\delta) +O(\epsilon^3).} \tag{4} \]

The variance is over candidate next tokens under the early predictor, at the same prefix. The constant and first-order terms cancel: the local log-normalizer starts at second order in the logit difference.

Proof of Equation (4)

Fix a prefix \(y_{<t}\). The local normalizer satisfies the exact identity

\[ \begin{aligned} \log C(y_{<t}) &=D_{\rm KL}\bigl(p_K(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\Vert q(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\bigr)\\ &\quad+wD_{\rm KL}\bigl(p_K(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\Vert p_E(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\bigr). \end{aligned} \tag{5} \]

Proof of Equation (5). For each possible next token \(y_t\), rearrange the definition of \(q(y_t\mid y_{<t})\):

\[ \log\frac{p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})}{q(y_t\mid y_{<t})} =\log C(y_{<t})-w\log\frac{p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})}{p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})}. \]

Taking the expectation over \(y_t\sim p_K(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\) yields Equation (5). For \(w\geq0\), both KL terms are nonnegative, so \(\log C(y_{<t})\geq0\). This identity does not require small logit differences. \(\square\)

For the Taylor expansion, write the logit difference as \(\delta(y_t\mid y_{<t})=z_K(y_t\mid y_{<t})-z_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})\). Define its log moment-generating function under the reference distribution:

\[ \psi(\alpha;y_{<t}) =\log\sum_{y_t}p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})e^{\alpha\delta(y_t\mid y_{<t})}. \]

Softmax gives

\[ p_K(y_t\mid y_{<t}) =p_E(y_t\mid y_{<t})e^{\delta(y_t\mid y_{<t})-\psi(1;y_{<t})}. \]

Substituting into the definition of \(C(y_{<t})\), we obtain the exact expression

\[ \log C(y_{<t}) =\psi(1+w;y_{<t})-(1+w)\psi(1;y_{<t}). \]

For \(\delta=\epsilon d\) with uniformly bounded \(d\), and bounded \(\alpha\), expand

\[ \begin{aligned} \psi(\alpha;y_{<t}) &=\alpha\mathbb E_{p_E(\cdot\mid y_{<t})}[\delta]\\ &\quad+\frac{\alpha^2}{2}\operatorname{Var}_{p_E(\cdot\mid y_{<t})}(\delta) +O(\epsilon^3). \end{aligned} \]

The expectation and variance are over next tokens \(y_t\) at the same prefix \(y_{<t}\). Substitution cancels the linear terms and leaves

\[ \begin{aligned} \log C(y_{<t}) &=\frac{(1+w)^2-(1+w)}{2}\operatorname{Var}_{p_E(\cdot\mid y_{<t})}(\delta) +O(\epsilon^3)\\ &=\frac{w(1+w)}{2}\operatorname{Var}_{p_E(\cdot\mid y_{<t})}(\delta) +O(\epsilon^3). \end{aligned} \]

This proves Equation (4). \(\square\)

To connect this local result to the sequence distribution, suppose \(0\leq\log C(y_{<t})\leq\eta\) at every prefix of a length-\(T\) sequence. Equation (3) then gives

\[ \left|\log\frac{Q_{\rm local}(y)}{Q_{\rm joint}(y)}\right| \leq T\eta. \tag{6} \]

Under the uniform small-difference assumption, \(\eta=O(\epsilon^2)\), so the sequence log-probability mismatch is \(O(T\epsilon^2)\). The global normalizer is included in this bound.

Proof of Equation (6)

Set \(D(y)=\prod_t C(y_{<t})\). Equation (3) gives \(ZQ_{\rm joint}(y)=Q_{\rm local}(y)D(y)\). Summing over sequences gives

\[ Z=\sum_y Q_{\rm local}(y)D(y) =\mathbb E_{Q_{\rm local}}D(Y). \]

The prefix bound gives \(1\leq D(y)\leq e^{T\eta}\) for every sequence. Its expectation obeys the same bounds, so \(0\leq\log Z\leq T\eta\). Finally,

\[ \log\frac{Q_{\rm local}(y)}{Q_{\rm joint}(y)} =\log Z-\log D(y). \]

Both terms lie in \([0,T\eta]\), establishing Equation (6). The bound includes both the local normalizers and the global normalizer. \(\square\)

Guidance changes token preferences at first order in \(\epsilon\), while the sequence mismatch accumulates at second order, \(O(T\epsilon^2)\). This is the useful separation: a nonzero guidance adjustment can coexist with a smaller normalization error.

04Are loop predictions actually closer?

We compared Huginn-0125 and Ouro-2.6B with two ordinary Transformers, Qwen2.5-3B and Pythia-2.8B. Each model saw the same text prefixes. For each model, we kept its final depth fixed and asked: how much do the next-token logits change between an intermediate depth and the final one? Depth means completed loops for a looped model and completed layers for an ordinary Transformer.

The horizontal axis below is the fraction of computation completed: 50% means halfway through that model's loops or layers. Lower values mean the intermediate and final logits are closer. The right panel also adjusts for differences in final-logit scale.

The looped models' mean gaps are 2.8–14.1 times smaller across all twelve comparisons. Every paired 95% bootstrap interval for the ratio lies below one. The ordering also holds after scale normalization and when using only question prefixes, before any answer text.

To measure this gap, let \(k\) be the intermediate depth, \(K\) the fixed final depth, \(V\) the vocabulary size, and \(v\) a candidate token's index. At each prefix, set \(\delta^{(k)}_v=z_{K,v}-z_{k,v}\) and remove its vocabulary mean, \(\bar\delta^{(k)}=V^{-1}\sum_v\delta^{(k)}_v\). We then compute

\[ \operatorname{RMS}_{\rm centered}(k,y_{<t}) =\sqrt{\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^V \bigl(\delta^{(k)}_v(y_{<t})-\bar\delta^{(k)}(y_{<t})\bigr)^2}. \tag{7} \]

This is the standard deviation of the logit changes across the vocabulary. Removing the mean ignores shifts that add the same constant to every token, since those do not change softmax. The plot averages this quantity over matched prefixes.

We used 1,024 text prefixes: eight positions on each of 128 GSM8K development questions. At every prefix, each intermediate prediction is compared with that model's own final prediction. The plotted positions are \(k/K\):

Our measurements show smaller intermediate-to-final logit gaps in the looped models we tested. This supports the small-difference assumption behind our analysis, connecting similar loop predictions to a smaller normalization mismatch in token-level guidance.

05The connection to diffusion

The extrapolation in Equation (1) also appears in diffusion guidance. Classifier-free guidance7 strengthens a prompt-conditioned prediction relative to an unconditional reference. The algebra is familiar, but the role of the normalizer differs.

Same reweighting idea, different treatment of the normalizer. At a fixed diffusion noise level, write \(p(x\mid c)\) for the conditional density and \(p(x)\) for the unconditional one. Guidance tilts their ratio:

\[ \widetilde p_w(x\mid c) =\frac{1}{Z_w(c)} \frac{p(x\mid c)^{1+w}}{p(x)^w}. \tag{8} \]

Diffusion uses a score, the gradient of a log density. Differentiation gives

\[ \nabla_x\log\widetilde p_w(x\mid c) =(1+w)\nabla_x\log p(x\mid c)-w\nabla_x\log p(x). \tag{9} \]

The normalizer vanishes because it does not depend on the sample coordinates \(x\).8*

Token-level guidance does not remove its prefix-dependent normalizers this way. Instead, close loop logits can make their effect small. Autoguidance9 makes the analogy more direct: it guides a diffusion model using a weaker version of a model, while loop guidance obtains its reference from an earlier stage of the same computation.

Connections between loops and diffusion also extend to how computation is trained. Time-Modulated Looped Transformers10 borrow diffusion-style timestep conditioning; LoopFormer11 adds step-size conditioning and shortcut consistency. Denoising Recursion Models12 learn correction of corrupted solutions, and Thinking with Looped Flows13 couples recurrent states to a denoising flow. In the other direction, Looped Diffusion Language Models14 and Looped-DiT15 repeat Transformer blocks inside diffusion models.

Test-time scaling is about how computation is organized, not only how much is spent.16 Loops refine a hidden state; diffusion refines a sample. Guidance adds another use for their intermediate predictions: a direction for correction. More compute is useful when the inference algorithm knows what to do with it. What scales is the model together with that algorithm.

* Score-based models learn scores of noisy distributions from samples.17 Equation (9) applies the reweighting identity at a fixed noise level. Using these guided scores throughout denoising need not sample the corresponding reweighted clean-data distribution: reweighting and adding noise do not generally commute. Sander's correction8, Bradley and Nakkiran18, and Chidambaram et al.19 discuss this distinction. Score-level CFG is therefore a practical implementation of the reweighting idea, not an exact clean-data sampling guarantee. ↩
