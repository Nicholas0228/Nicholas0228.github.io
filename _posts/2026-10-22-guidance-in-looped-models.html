Guidance is now part of the standard recipe for image and video generation. It can improve prompt following and sample quality by changing how a trained model is sampled, often at the cost of diversity.1 Stable Diffusion 32 and Wan3 use it, while FLUX.1 [dev]4 distills it into the model.

In language models, there is a catch: guiding each token is not generally the same as guiding the whole answer. This post explores how loops can narrow that gap. When their readouts are sufficiently close, the normalization error starts at second order in the logit difference. We will work through why, then check how close looped-model readouts are in practice.

01Diffusion gets to ignore a normalizer

Start with a conditional density \(p(x\mid c)\), where \(c\) might be a text prompt. Suppose we want to favor samples that are especially characteristic of that condition. One way to express this preference is to tilt the density:

\[ \widetilde p_w(x\mid c) =\frac{1}{Z_w(c)}\, \frac{p(x\mid c)^{1+w}}{p(x)^w},\qquad w\geq0. \tag{1} \]

The ratio \(p(x\mid c)/p(x)\) rewards samples that the condition helps explain. At \(w=0\), we recover ordinary conditional generation. Increasing \(w\) strengthens that preference. The denominator \(Z_w(c)\) makes the density integrate to one.

Diffusion uses the corresponding construction at each noise level \(t\). With \(x_t\) denoting the whole noisy sample, take the log-gradient of the tilted density:

\[ \nabla_{x_t}\log\widetilde p_{t,w}(x_t\mid c) =(1+w)\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t\mid c) -w\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t). \tag{2} \]

This is the score combination behind classifier-free guidance1. The normalizer depends on the condition and noise level, but not on the sample coordinates \(x_t\). Its gradient is zero. We can therefore evaluate the tilted score without evaluating the integral defining \(Z_{t,w}\).

Show the density-to-score derivation

At a fixed noise level, assume differentiable positive densities and a finite normalizer. Taking logarithms of the noisy version of Equation (1) gives

\[ \log\widetilde p_{t,w} =(1+w)\log p_t(x_t\mid c)-w\log p_t(x_t)-\log Z_{t,w}(c). \]

Differentiate with respect to \(x_t\). Since \(Z_{t,w}(c)\) is constant in that variable, the last term disappears, giving Equation (2). Bayes' rule also gives

\[ \frac{p_t(x_t\mid c)}{p_t(x_t)} =\frac{p_t(c\mid x_t)}{p(c)}. \]

Thus the same tilt can be written as \(p_t(x_t\mid c)\,p_t(c\mid x_t)^w\), up to a factor depending only on \(c\). With exact scores, this is an exact identity at that noise level.

There is a subtlety here: an exact tilted score at each noise level does not by itself imply that the complete diffusion sampler produces the clean-data tilt in Equation (1). Sander Dieleman added this qualification in the May 29 correction to his guidance post5; later analyses by Bradley and Nakkiran6 and Chidambaram et al.7 make the distinction precise. What we use here is the score identity at a fixed noise level.

02Tokens make that normalizer matter

We can perform a very similar subtraction with an LLM's next-token logits. Contrastive decoding8 uses an expert and an amateur model; DoLa9 reads predictions from different layers. The reference and decoding constraints vary, but the idea of contrasting predictions already has a history in language modeling.

The complication is that a next-token distribution is conditional on what has already been generated. In his 2022 post, Sander put his intuition this way:

“my hunch is that diffusion guidance works so well precisely because we are able to apply it to the joint distribution”

Sander Dieleman, Guidance: a cheat code for diffusion models5 (2022)

His contrast was with modifying sequential conditionals individually. We can make that distinction explicit with two predictors: a stronger one \(p_K\) and a reference \(p_E\). Both see the same prefix \(s\). Suppress the fixed prompt in the notation, assume positive token probabilities, and define

\[ a(v\mid s)=\frac{p_K(v\mid s)^{1+w}}{p_E(v\mid s)^w}, \qquad C(s)=\sum_v a(v\mid s). \]

Local guidance samples the next token from \(q(v\mid s)=a(v\mid s)/C(s)\). This is exactly what softmax of \((1+w)z_K-wz_E\) computes. Now consider a complete answer \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)\), with a fixed length \(T\). Define its unnormalized guided weight as

\[ A(y)=\prod_{t=1}^T a(y_t\mid y_{<t}) =\frac{P_K(y)^{1+w}}{P_E(y)^w}. \]

Guiding the whole answer normalizes these weights once. Guiding each token normalizes at every prefix:

\[ Q_{\rm joint}(y)=\frac{A(y)}{Z}, \qquad Q_{\rm local}(y)=\frac{A(y)}{\prod_{t=1}^T C(y_{<t})}, \quad Z=\sum_y A(y). \tag{3} \]

The numerators agree. The denominators generally do not: different answers visit different prefixes, and can accumulate different normalizing factors. Those factors change the relative probability of complete answers. This is why a valid guided distribution for each next token need not implement the intended guided distribution over answers.

The missing term looks into the future

Every joint distribution admits a sequential factorization. The issue is whether the simple local rule gives us the right conditionals. Let \(H(s)\) be the total unnormalized guided weight of every possible completion after prefix \(s\). The exact conditional of the joint target is

\[ Q_{\rm joint}(v\mid s) =\frac{a(v\mid s)H(sv)}{\sum_u a(u\mid s)H(su)}. \tag{4} \]

The term \(H(sv)\) accounts for all the futures opened up by choosing \(v\). Ordinary local guidance omits it. Computing it exactly is usually impractical, because it requires summing over possible continuations.

Show the exact sequential factorization

Let \(A_{\rm past}(s)\) be the product of guided weights along the prefix. Define \(H(s)\) as the sum of the remaining products over completions of total length \(T\), with \(H(y)=1\) for a complete answer. It satisfies

\[ H(s)=\sum_v a(v\mid s)H(sv),\qquad H(\varnothing)=Z. \]

The joint target's marginal prefix mass is \(Q_{\rm joint}(s)=A_{\rm past}(s)H(s)/Z\). Divide the mass of \(sv\) by the mass of \(s\):

\[ \frac{Q_{\rm joint}(sv)}{Q_{\rm joint}(s)} =\frac{a(v\mid s)H(sv)}{H(s)}. \]

This gives Equation (4). The local rule agrees whenever \(H(sv)\) is the same for every candidate \(v\) at that prefix. A shared network or a shared output head does not alone enforce this condition.

A tiny example makes the difference visible

Suppose an answer has just two tokens. Both predictors choose \(A\) or \(B\) with probability \(1/2\). After \(A\), both remain uniform over \(1,2\). After \(B\), the reference is still uniform but the stronger predictor assigns probabilities \(0.9,0.1\). Local guidance cannot change the first decision: the predictors agree there. The joint target can, because it sees the guided weight of the continuations after \(B\).

03Loooooop

A looped Transformer reuses the same block of layers several times before predicting the next token. Huginn has three parts: prelude layers process the prefix once, a recurrent core repeatedly refines a hidden state, and coda layers read out the result.10 Ouro instead repeats its full decoder stack.11

For a fixed prefix, write one application of the shared block as \(f\); any fixed input from the prelude is implicit. The recurrence looks like this:

\(h_E\)Early state
→
\(f(h_E)\)One more loop
→
\(f(f(h_E))\)Same block again
⋯ →
\(h_K\)Final state

Apply the same output layers to \(h_E\) and \(h_K\), including the coda where present, and we get early and final logits \(z_E\) and \(z_K\). The network supplies its own reference.

LoopCD12 uses these recurrent readouts for contrastive decoding without additional training. Its logit variant extrapolates from the early prediction toward the later one:

\[ z_{\rm guided}=z_K+w(z_K-z_E),\qquad q=\operatorname{softmax}(z_{\rm guided}). \tag{5} \]

The paper's standard logit reference is the first loop, so the two readouts need not come from adjacent iterations. It also studies hidden-state guidance; our probability argument below concerns the logit variant. Passing a hidden-state combination through nonlinear output layers need not give Equation (5).

The reported gains can be substantial. At matched recurrent depth, adaptive LoopCD-Logits raises Ouro-2.6B-Thinking's AIME 2024 pass@1 from 61.88% to 73.33%; LoopCD-Hidden raises Huginn's HumanEval pass@1 from 22.56% to 31.71% at 32 loops (Tables 1 and 3).12 In a separate seven-benchmark multiple-choice comparison, half-depth guidance matches or exceeds the full-depth mean with 22.5–48.2% fewer theoretical forward FLOPs (Table A9). Gains depend on the setting: fixed logit guidance lowers Huginn's GSM8K accuracy from 42.91% to 41.62% (Table A5).

Why might this be useful beyond making the reference convenient? Reusing the block and readout makes it plausible that early and late predictions remain compatible. That suggests an empirical question: are their logits actually closer than predictions read from different layers of an ordinary LLM? If so, we have a reason to examine the small-difference limit of the normalization problem.

What cancels when the readouts are close?

At a fixed prefix, write \(\delta=z_K-z_E\). The exact local normalizer has a useful interpretation:

\[ \log C(s) =D_{\rm KL}(p_K\Vert q)+wD_{\rm KL}(p_K\Vert p_E). \tag{6} \]

For \(w\geq0\), both terms are nonnegative. The normalizer accounts for disagreement between the readouts and the extra change introduced by guidance.

Derive the exact KL identity

Suppress the prefix and use \(\log q=(1+w)\log p_K-w\log p_E-\log C\). Rearranging gives

\[ \log\frac{p_K(v)}{q(v)} =\log C-w\log\frac{p_K(v)}{p_E(v)}. \]

Take the expectation over \(v\sim p_K\). The left side becomes \(D_{\rm KL}(p_K\Vert q)\), while the final expectation is \(D_{\rm KL}(p_K\Vert p_E)\). Moving that term to the left yields Equation (6). This identity is exact and does not require small logit differences.

Now assume that, after removing an irrelevant constant offset, \(\delta=\epsilon d\), with \(d\) bounded and \(\epsilon\) small. Keep the guidance strength fixed and bounded. A Taylor expansion gives

\[ \boxed{\log C(s) =\frac{w(1+w)}{2}\operatorname{Var}_{p_E}(\delta) +O(\epsilon^3).} \tag{7} \]

The constant and first-order terms cancel. Guidance changes token log-odds at first order; its local log-normalization cost starts at second order.

This is the cancellation that makes the connection interesting. The normalization factor has not vanished exactly. Its leading contribution is quadratic in the logit difference.

Work through the Taylor expansion

Define the log moment-generating function of the logit difference under the reference distribution:

\[ \psi(\alpha)=\log\mathbb E_{v\sim p_E}e^{\alpha\delta(v)}. \]

Softmax gives \(p_K(v)=p_E(v)e^{\delta(v)-\psi(1)}\). Substituting into the definition of \(C\), we obtain the exact expression

\[ \log C=\psi(1+w)-(1+w)\psi(1). \]

For \(\delta=\epsilon d\) with uniformly bounded \(d\), and bounded \(\alpha\), expand

\[ \psi(\alpha)=\alpha\mu+ \frac{\alpha^2}{2}\sigma^2+O(\epsilon^3), \quad \mu=\mathbb E_{p_E}\delta, \quad \sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}_{p_E}(\delta). \]

The two linear terms are both \((1+w)\mu\). Their difference is zero; the remaining quadratic coefficient is

\[ \frac{(1+w)^2-(1+w)}{2}\sigma^2 =\frac{w(1+w)}{2}\sigma^2. \]

Adding any constant to \(\delta\) adds a linear term to \(\psi\), which cancels in exactly the same way. The relevant assumption concerns variation across tokens, not the arbitrary origin of the logits.

For comparison, the change in log-odds relative to the final predictor is exactly

\[ \log\frac{q(v)}{q(u)}-\log\frac{p_K(v)}{p_K(u)} =w\bigl(\delta(v)-\delta(u)\bigr). \]

For fixed \(w\), this is \(O(\epsilon)\), whereas \(\log C\) begins at \(O(\epsilon^2)\).

From a local cancellation to a joint approximation

To return to whole answers, suppose \(0\leq\log C(s)\leq\eta\) at every relevant prefix. For fixed-length answers, Equation (3) then implies

\[ \left|\log\frac{Q_{\rm local}(y)}{Q_{\rm joint}(y)}\right| \leq T\eta. \tag{8} \]

If the small-difference assumption holds uniformly, then \(\eta=O(\epsilon^2)\). The sequence-level mismatch is therefore \(O(T\epsilon^2)\). This gives the connection we were looking for: a regime in which the simple sequential rule approximates the globally tilted joint distribution, without explicitly evaluating the future-completion weights.

Prove the sequence bound, including the global normalizer

Set \(D(y)=\prod_t C(y_{<t})\). Since \(A(y)=Q_{\rm local}(y)D(y)\), summing over answers gives

\[ Z=\sum_y Q_{\rm local}(y)D(y) =\mathbb E_{Q_{\rm local}}D(Y). \]

The prefix bound gives \(1\leq D(y)\leq e^{T\eta}\) for every answer. Its expectation obeys the same bounds, so \(0\leq\log Z\leq T\eta\). Finally,

\[ \log\frac{Q_{\rm local}(y)}{Q_{\rm joint}(y)} =\log Z-\log D(y). \]

Both terms lie in \([0,T\eta]\), establishing Equation (8). This argument retains the global normalizer rather than dropping it.

A finite, non-asymptotic bound is also available. Let \(R=\max_v\delta(v)-\min_v\delta(v)\). In the preceding proof, \(\psi''(\alpha)\) is the variance of \(\delta\) under an exponentially tilted distribution, and is at most \(R^2/4\). The remainder for linear interpolation of \(\psi\) at 0 and 1, evaluated at \(1+w\), gives

\[ 0\leq\log C\leq\frac{w(1+w)}{8}R^2. \]

Thus a uniform range bound \(R(s)\leq R_*\) yields \(\eta=w(1+w)R_*^2/8\). This makes clear why average RMS alone cannot certify the sequence guarantee. The proof uses a fixed finite horizon and common positive support; unrestricted generation with EOS needs corresponding control of length and the partition function.

The condition is stronger than having a small average discrepancy on a dataset. Long answers accumulate the residual, and large guidance strengths amplify it. The mathematics also applies to any pair of sufficiently close predictors. Loops are a possible way to obtain such a pair, not an extra algebraic assumption.

04Are loop readouts actually closer?

This is where a numerical check helps. We compared two looped models, Huginn-0125 and Ouro-2.6B, with two ordinary Transformers, Qwen2.5-3B and Pythia-2.8B. Each model saw the same 1,024 text prefixes: eight positions on each of 128 GSM8K development questions. For each prefix, we compared an intermediate readout with that model's own final readout.

The main measurement is the vocabulary RMS of the logit difference after removing its mean. This removes an arbitrary offset that has no effect on softmax:

\[ \operatorname{RMS}_{\rm centered}(\delta) =\sqrt{\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^V(\delta_v-\bar\delta)^2}, \qquad \bar\delta=\frac{1}{V}\sum_v\delta_v. \]

We compare readouts a quarter, halfway, and three quarters of the way through each model. For Huginn these are loops 8, 16, and 24 out of 32; for Ouro, loops 1, 2, and 3 out of 4. The corresponding layer indices are 9, 18, and 27 out of 36 for Qwen, and 8, 16, and 24 out of 32 for Pythia.

The looped models' mean gaps are 2.8–14.1 times smaller across the twelve loop-versus-layer comparisons at 25%, 50%, and 75% readout depth. Every paired 95% bootstrap interval for the ratio lies below one. At half depth, for example, Huginn's gap is 0.268 times Pythia's, with interval [0.261, 0.276]; Ouro's is 0.203 times Pythia's, with interval [0.199, 0.209]. The ordering survives both the scale normalization in the right panel and a check using only the question prefixes, before any answer text.

Read the measurement protocol and its scope

The models are frozen base checkpoints. Inputs use a common plain-text, eight-shot Q/A format. Answer-prefix positions come from cached Huginn-32 continuations, with no filtering for answer correctness. We selected shared character boundaries that round-trip exactly through all four tokenizers, including the prompt boundary. This matches text, not token IDs across different vocabularies.

Intermediate states use each architecture's native final normalization and output head, including Huginn's coda. We did not fit a tuned lens. Native forwards use BF16; full-vocabulary statistics use float64 at temperature one. A native-forward check and a future-token control passed at all 84 audited model/depth settings. An independent audit recomputed 28,672 depth-position records.

Intervals resample the 128 questions, retaining each question's eight positions together; paired comparisons resample the same questions for both models. We used 5,000 bootstrap draws. These are individual intervals over the sampled questions, not simultaneous intervals or estimates of variation across training seeds.

The normalized gap divides by the final readout's centered RMS at each position before averaging. Both plot axes start at zero. Lines connect the measured depths; the final-depth zero is omitted because it is true by definition.

Depth fractions are a comparison convention, not equal FLOPs or equal predictor competence. The models differ in architecture, training, vocabulary, and size. Their gaps support a statement about these checkpoints and readout procedures; they do not isolate weight sharing as the cause. Ordinary layers may also benefit from a trained intermediate readout.

LoopCD's first-loop reference deserves separate care: it is 1/32 of the deployed depth for Huginn but 1/4 for Ouro. We therefore do not present “first versus final” as a matched-depth comparison. The source report includes that diagnostic separately.

These results support the comparative premise: for these models, loop readouts are much closer to the final logits than ordinary intermediate-layer readouts are. They do not yet certify the uniform small-difference assumption used in the sequence bound. In particular, smaller RMS need not make the quadratic expansion numerically accurate at every prefix.

How accurate is the quadratic approximation itself?

We also computed the exact \(\log C\) and its quadratic approximation. At a quarter depth with \(w=0.5\), the median relative error across the 1,024 positions is 76.9% for Huginn and 75.3% for Ouro, versus 29.2% for Qwen and 27.6% for Pythia. Here relative error means \(|\text{quadratic}-\log C|/\max(\log C,10^{-12})\); the floor is inactive at these positions. The looped models have smaller absolute normalizers in this comparison, but not smaller relative Taylor error.

Nor are all tokenwise gaps tiny: even at three-quarter depth, the largest observed absolute centered logit difference is 5.60 for Huginn and 6.44 for Ouro. A small vocabulary-average gap can coexist with larger differences on particular tokens. The empirical result is therefore evidence for closer readouts, while the joint-distribution guarantee remains conditional on a stronger bound.

05Beyond guidance

Guidance is one of several ideas that can travel between diffusion and looped models. In autoguidance, Karras et al. guide a diffusion model with a smaller, less-trained version of itself.13 The useful reference is a weaker prediction of the same thing. Loops offer an economical way to obtain one: stop the same computation earlier.

Another connection is to give each loop a sense of where it is along a computation. LoopFormer conditions its shared block on an internal time \(t\) and step size \(\Delta t\), borrowing from diffusion timestep conditioning and shortcut models. It trains shorter trajectories to match the predictions of longer ones, allowing the same model to run at different compute budgets.14

Thinking with Looped Flows brings flow modeling into recurrent reasoning itself.15 It trains a recurrent denoiser on progressively less noisy versions of a solution. At inference, a flow turns noise into a solution while the recurrent state carries information between steps. A finer time grid provides more computation; different initial noise samples can produce different valid solutions.

These approaches share a practical question: how should predictions improve as we spend more computation? Here, we used intermediate predictions to understand guidance. Time conditioning, shortcut training, and denoising also let us shape how those predictions are produced. Guidance may be an early example of a much wider exchange between diffusion and recurrent reasoning.
